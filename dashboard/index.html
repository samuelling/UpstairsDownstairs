Upstairs/Downstairs

When a demanding task occupies central vision, how does the brain redistribute its resources between the attention network, the attended location and the periphery?

A reanalysis of the NJP central-load fMRI study: 8 subjects and 151 task runs. Subjects watch a little ring of red and blue squares and report whenever the majority color flips. Load is the minority share, from 10% (easy) to 46% (nearly 50/50). Meanwhile a task-irrelevant noise annulus fills the periphery (3–7.5°). Responses come from the original per-subject FS-FAST GLMs on fsaverage.

Summary

Harder central judgments turn the brain's attention network up, but barely turn the periphery down

Not yet controlled: load is confounded with run position (one ratio per run, in a fixed order). A refit with run position as a covariate is the next step, and it could change these numbers.

  1. Load degrades performance. At 46%, subjects detect about half of the flips, compared with all of them at 10–30%.
  2. Dorsal attention activity scales with load. Every IPS, SPL and FEF region rises with load, across three atlases; IPS2 gains about 1.2% signal. The default mode network declines.
  3. The scaling is linear. Over 10–46% the response rises linearly, with no evidence of saturation, although 7 levels limit how well saturation can be detected.
  4. Peripheral suppression is weak. Foveal V1–V3 increase with load; peripheral V1–V3 only trend downward (V2, uncorrected).
  5. Effects are right-lateralized. Load effects in attention and control networks are larger on the right, alongside a larger right-hemisphere response overall.

By the numbers

Part one

The experiment

What subjects did in the scanner, and whether the difficulty manipulation worked.

01
Paradigm

A numerosity judgment at fixation, with parametric load

Subjects fixated a central dot surrounded by a ring of 173 small squares, each red or blue, between 0.5° and 1.5° eccentricity. The squares reshuffled to new random positions about twice a second. Every 5–6 reshuffles the majority color was redrawn at random; when it changed, subjects had 1.5 s to report the new majority color. That produced 2–3 flips per 16-s block.

Load was set by the share of the minority color. At 10% the majority is obvious at a glance; at 46% the ring is close to an even mix and each judgment requires sustained, effortful integration. During every task block, a task-irrelevant 1/f noise annulus filled the periphery (3–7.5°). It was identical across load levels, which lets us ask whether its cortical representation is suppressed as central demands rise.

The demo shows the 2016–17 setup used for 6 of the 8 subjects: 173 squares, red at RGB 185, 56 px per degree. S12 and S13 were scanned in 2018 on a newer display: 185 squares, red at RGB 255, and the noise texture fixed at 0° and refreshed at 15 Hz. Stimulus sizes in degrees match across setups.

02
Manipulation check

Harder ratios cost detections more than accuracy

Takeaway The load manipulation works in all 8 subjects. From the 30% to the 46% ring, the task score falls from about 95% to 33%. Most of that drop comes from flips that go unnoticed (detected 100% → 53%). Flips that are caught are still usually answered correctly (96% → 66%).

How this was measured

After each majority-color flip, subjects had 1.5 s (1.0 s in the earliest script version, used for S4 and part of S3) to press the key for the new majority color. The data were scored three ways. The task score is the original measure: correct / all flips, so misses count as wrong. Detection is the % of flips answered. Accuracy is the % correct among answered flips. S12 and S13 pressed reversed keys in every run (raw accuracy 0–46%), so their mapping is flipped. They are also the only two subjects scanned in 2018 on a newer display setup, so a different button-box mapping is the likely cause. Misses stay wrong, as in the original code.

What the brain did

03
Upstairs

Dorsal attention activity scales with load

Takeaway Every dorsal attention area rises as the ring gets harder (t = 4.6–8.1). The rise is largest in IPS2–IPS3 (about +1.2% signal from 10% to 46%) and smallest in FEF. Most areas peak at 44% and dip slightly at 46%, where subjects miss half of the flips.
How this was tested

Responses were averaged within Wang 2015 maximum-probability ROIs, with hemispheres averaged. Each subject's response was regressed on ratio, and the slopes were t-tested across subjects. The "fitted" value is slope × 0.36, the predicted change from 10% to 46%. Units are roughly % signal change vs fixation. X positions are evenly spaced but the ratios are not; the fits use the true ratios.

03b
Robustness

The effect replicates across atlases; the default mode network declines

Takeaway The rise in IPS, SPL and FEF doesn't depend on how the brain is parcellated. It holds in Glasser (IPS1, MIP, VIP, 7PL, LIP, FEF, PEF) and in Schaefer (dorsal attention SPL and FEF, control PFCd, anterior insula). Default mode A goes down with load. MT shows no load effect.

How this was tested

The same slope test was run for every ROI in each atlas, with FDR correction within each atlas. A filled dot means q < .05; small dots are individual subjects. Click a row to plot its curve on the right.

04
Downstairs

Foveal cortex increases; peripheral suppression is weak

Takeaway Visual cortex representing the task ring (the fovea) rises with load in V1, V2 and V3 (p = .003–.007). Visual cortex representing the noise annulus (the periphery) trends down in all three areas. Only V2 reaches p < .05, and it doesn't survive FDR. So the predicted suppression of the periphery is, at most, weak. Subject-specific pRF maps and the anatomical template give the same answer.
How this was tested

Each subject's pRF eccentricity, R² and V1–V3 labels were resampled to fsaverage with FreeSurfer 6. Each area was split into a fovea band (0.5–1.5°, the task ring) and a periphery band (3–7.5°, the noise annulus), keeping vertices with pRF R² > 10%. S9 has no pRF data, so the Benson 2014 template within Wang V1–V3 stands in for that subject. The "Template" tab uses the template for everyone.

05
Load function

Load responses are linear, not saturating

Takeaway In IPS0–IPS4/5, SPL1, FEF, LO and dorsal attention A, a load function decisively beats a flat line (summed ΔAICc 33–64; 5–8 of 8 subjects each). About 60–75% of their vertices prefer a load function, and about 95% of those increase. The winning shape is linear, not saturating: even the group-average curves choose linear. One limit: with only 7 load levels, a saturating curve can't be reliably told apart from a straight line (see the simulation below).

How this was tested, and how far 7 load levels can be trusted

The 7 load levels were fit with four models, with load rescaled to 0–1 across 10% → 46%: flat (1 parameter); linear (2); saturating exponential a + s·(1−e−x/τ), which becomes linear as τ grows (3); and Naka-Rushton a + s·xⁿ/(xⁿ+c50ⁿ) (4). The amplitude s can be negative, so decreases count too. Nonlinear parameters were grid-searched, and amplitude and offset solved by least squares. Models were compared with AICc, which penalizes extra parameters and matters with only 7 points. Fits ran (a) at every fsaverage vertex in each subject and (b) on each subject's ROI average and on the group-mean curve.

06
Lateralization

Load effects are right-lateralized

Takeaway Load effects are larger on the right in the dorsal attention network (+0.32%, p = .035, 6 of 8 subjects) and the control network (p = .017; only the right side is significant on its own). The right hemisphere also responds about 35% more overall, and relative to that baseline the load effects are similar on both sides (0.76 vs 0.82). So this looks like the task engaging the right hemisphere more, not a right-only load mechanism. Default mode shows no asymmetry, and V1–V3 show only a tiny one.

How this was tested

Each subject's load slope was computed separately for the left and right hemisphere of every ROI. Right vs left was tested with a paired t-test (the hemisphere × load interaction), with FDR within each atlas. Individually, IPS0 (q = .045) and Glasser IP2 (q = .046) survive FDR. Because single ROIs are noisy, the composites average each subject's per-ROI effects first. For per-ROI views, use the hemisphere control above sections 03–05.

07
Interpretation

Interpretation and open questions

Where things stand

Making a small central judgment harder recruits the dorsal attention network in a graded, roughly linear way. It also raises activity in the visual cortex that represents the task ring and lowers activity in the default mode network. That fits the "upstairs" half of the story well.

The "downstairs" half, where the periphery is suppressed as the center demands more, is weak in this dataset. Peripheral V1–V3 trend down, but the effect is small and not reliable after correction. The task also loads the right hemisphere more than the left.

What could still change the picture

  • Run order. Each run used one ratio, and each subject repeated a single ratio order, so slow drift within a session could look like a load effect, or hide one. This is the biggest open issue.
  • Disengagement at 46%. Subjects miss about half the flips at 46%, which may mean they partly give up. That could explain the dip at the hardest level.
  • Stimulus differences. The foveal increase could partly reflect the different red/blue mixes or the number of button presses, not attention alone.

Next steps

  • Refit the GLMs with run position as a covariate to separate load from run order. This is the next analysis; it needs a cluster batch job.
  • Link to behavior in the periphery. An earlier psychophysics study used the same kind of central task with a peripheral tilt-discrimination task. It could show whether peripheral perception actually suffers as central load rises.

Appendix

Methods, data checks & provenance

  • Data & label audit. Each scan run's condition file (CAM.par) was checked against the ratio stored in its behavioral file: of runs match. Raw f.nii, motion-corrected and surface-sampled data exist for every run, so a full rerun from raw data is possible. Preprocessing and GLM settings were confirmed from the original FS-FAST configs: fsaverage surface, 5 mm FWHM smoothing, SPM HRF, 16 s blocks, TR 2 s.
  • Two display setups. S1–S11 were scanned in 2016–17 (41.9 cm screen at 104 cm), and S12 and S13 in 2018 on a newer setup (51.2 cm at 82 cm). The 2018 setup differs in square count (185 vs 173), red luminance (RGB 255 vs 185), noise refresh and orientation, and button mapping. Stimulus sizes in degrees match, but setup is confounded with these two subjects. Their load effects look like everyone else's, as the per-subject dots in the ROI chart show.
  • Units. FS-FAST betas after normalization to a mean of 100, so roughly % signal change relative to fixation. Wang IPS4 and IPS5 are merged (IPS5 is only 18–53 vertices).
  • Model comparison power. With 7 levels and per-subject noise, AICc is conservative. Foveal V1–V3 pass the group slope test but count as "flat" by per-subject AICc, because the effect is consistent across subjects but small relative to each subject's noise. Vertex maps (ΔAICc, τ, number of subjects with ΔAICc > 2) are saved as fsaverage .mgz files on the cluster.
  • Task reconstruction. The animation in section 01 is built from the original experiment code and the parameters stored in the behavior files. The noise updates more slowly than in the scanner unless "Real noise speed" is on.
  • Code. scripts/ contains the label audit, ROI extraction, pRF resampling, model fits, recovery simulation and this page's data build.
