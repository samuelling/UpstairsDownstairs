Upstairs/Downstairs

When a demanding task occupies central vision, how does the brain redistribute its resources between the attention network, the attended location and the periphery?

A reanalysis of the NJP central-load fMRI study: 8 subjects and 151 task runs. Subjects watch a little ring of red and blue squares and report whenever the majority color flips. Load is the minority share, from 10% (easy) to 46% (nearly 50/50). Meanwhile a task-irrelevant noise annulus fills the periphery (3–7.5°). Responses come from the original per-subject FS-FAST GLMs on fsaverage.

Summary

Harder central judgments turn the brain's attention network up, but barely turn the periphery down

Load was partly confounded with run position (one ratio per run, in a repeating order). A run-by-run refit that controls run position, scan day and motion leaves the load effects essentially unchanged (section 03c).

  1. Load degrades performance. At 46%, subjects detect about half of the flips, compared with all of them at 10–30%.
  2. Dorsal attention activity scales with load. Every IPS, SPL and FEF region rises with load, across three atlases; IPS2 gains about 1.2% signal. The default mode network declines.
  3. The scaling is linear. Over 10–46% the response rises linearly with the minority share. A compressive, saturating response appears only when load is re-expressed as behavioral difficulty, and that description fits the data poorly.
  4. Peripheral suppression is modest and broad. Foveal V1–V3 increase with load. From about 3° outward, responses decrease, by about 0.4% at 6–9°, and the decrease extends beyond the stimulated annulus to 11°, so it is not specific to the ignored stimulus.
  5. Effects are right-lateralized. Load effects in attention and control networks are larger on the right, alongside a larger right-hemisphere response overall.

By the numbers

Part one

The experiment

What subjects did in the scanner, and whether the difficulty manipulation worked.

01
Paradigm

A numerosity judgment at fixation, with parametric load

Subjects fixated a central dot surrounded by a ring of 173 small squares, each red or blue, between 0.5° and 1.5° eccentricity. The squares reshuffled to new random positions about twice a second. Every 5–6 reshuffles the majority color was redrawn at random; when it changed, subjects had 1.5 s to report the new majority color. That produced 2–3 flips per 16-s block.

Load was set by the share of the minority color. At 10% the majority is obvious at a glance; at 46% the ring is close to an even mix and each judgment requires sustained, effortful integration. During every task block, a task-irrelevant 1/f noise annulus filled the periphery (3–7.5°). It was identical across load levels, which lets us ask whether its cortical representation is suppressed as central demands rise.

The demo shows the 2016–17 setup used for 6 of the 8 subjects: 173 squares, red at RGB 185, 56 px per degree. S12 and S13 were scanned in 2018 on a newer display: 185 squares, red at RGB 255, and the noise texture fixed at 0° and refreshed at 15 Hz. Stimulus sizes in degrees match across setups.

02
Manipulation check

Harder ratios cost detections more than accuracy

Takeaway The load manipulation works in all 8 subjects. From the 30% to the 46% ring, the task score falls from about 95% to 33%. Most of that drop comes from flips that go unnoticed (detected 100% → 53%). Flips that are caught are still usually answered correctly (96% → 66%).

How this was measured

After each majority-color flip, subjects had 1.5 s (1.0 s in the earliest script version, used for S4 and part of S3) to press the key for the new majority color. The data were scored three ways. The task score is the original measure: correct / all flips, so misses count as wrong. Detection is the % of flips answered. Accuracy is the % correct among answered flips. S12 and S13 pressed reversed keys in every run (raw accuracy 0–46%), so their mapping is flipped. They are also the only two subjects scanned in 2018 on a newer display setup, so a different button-box mapping is the likely cause. Misses stay wrong, as in the original code.

Part two

What the brain did

Three questions: what happens upstairs (attention networks), what goes downstairs (visual cortex for the center vs the periphery), and what shape the load response takes.

03
Upstairs

Dorsal attention activity scales with load

Takeaway Every dorsal attention area rises as the ring gets harder (t = 4.6–8.1). The rise is largest in IPS2–IPS3 (about +1.2% signal from 10% to 46%) and smallest in FEF. Most areas peak at 44% and dip slightly at 46%, where subjects miss half of the flips.
How this was tested

Responses were averaged within Wang 2015 maximum-probability ROIs, with hemispheres averaged. Each subject's response was regressed on ratio, and the slopes were t-tested across subjects. The "fitted" value is slope × 0.36, the predicted change from 10% to 46%. Units are roughly % signal change vs fixation. X positions are evenly spaced but the ratios are not; the fits use the true ratios.

03b
Robustness

The effect replicates across atlases; the default mode network declines

Takeaway The rise in IPS, SPL and FEF doesn't depend on how the brain is parcellated. It holds in Glasser (IPS1, MIP, VIP, 7PL, LIP, FEF, PEF) and in Schaefer (dorsal attention SPL and FEF, control PFCd, anterior insula). Default mode A goes down with load. MT shows no load effect.

How this was tested

To see where every ROI sits on the cortex, open the Brain maps tab. The same slope test was run for every ROI in each atlas, with FDR correction within each atlas. A filled dot means q < .05; small dots are individual subjects. Click a row to plot its curve on the right.

03c
Control

Run order does not explain the load effect

How this was tested

Each run was refit on its own: the smoothed, surface-sampled time series was intensity-normalized to a global mean of 100 (as in the original FS-FAST analysis), averaged within each ROI and hemisphere, and fit with the 16-s ON blocks convolved with the SPM canonical HRF plus quadratic drift (the original settings); a second fit added the six motion regressors. Per subject and ROI, the run betas (hemispheres averaged) were then regressed on load alone (uncontrolled, which reproduces the original slopes, r = .99 across subjects × ROIs), and on load plus scan-day terms plus run position within the day (controlled; only S3 was scanned on two days). Load slopes were t-tested across subjects with FDR within each atlas. Run-level betas are on a somewhat smaller scale than the FS-FAST betas, so compare the two estimates here with each other rather than with other sections. Load and run position were only weakly correlated within subjects (mean |r| = ).

04
Downstairs

Foveal cortex increases; the periphery decreases modestly

Takeaway Visual cortex representing the task ring (the fovea) rises with load in V1, V2 and V3 (p = .003–.007). Visual cortex representing the noise annulus (the periphery) trends down in all three areas. Only V2 reaches p < .05, and it doesn't survive FDR. So the predicted suppression of the periphery is, at most, weak. Subject-specific pRF maps and the anatomical template give the same answer.
How this was tested

Each subject's pRF eccentricity, R² and V1–V3 labels were resampled to fsaverage with FreeSurfer 6. Each area was split into a fovea band (0.5–1.5°, the task ring) and a periphery band (3–7.5°, the noise annulus), keeping vertices with pRF R² > 10%. S9 has no pRF data, so the Benson 2014 template within Wang V1–V3 stands in for that subject. The "Template" tab uses the template for everyone.

04b
Downstairs, mapped

The decrease grows with eccentricity and extends beyond the annulus

Load effect across eccentricity, V1–V3

Regions defined by the annulus localizer

How this was tested

Eccentricity profile. Within each subject's pRF-defined V1, V2 and V3 (pRF R² > 10%; S9 has no pRF data, so the Wang areas and Benson template eccentricity stand in), vertices were binned by pRF eccentricity from 0.25° to 14°. In each bin the 7 load betas from the original GLMs were averaged across vertices (hemispheres pooled), and the load slope was fit and expressed as the fitted change from 10% to 46% load. Areas were averaged within subject before testing; each bin is t-tested across subjects without correction for the number of bins.

Annulus localizer. Each subject also ran two localizer runs in which the same peripheral noise annulus alternated with fixation. Within V1–V3, vertices were classed as annulus-driven (On vs fixation, p < .001) or not driven (|sig| < 1), and driven vertices were split into thirds by localizer strength. Strength is confounded with eccentricity: the weakest-driven third sits near 2.5°, where the load effect crosses zero, so these thirds do not isolate a response-gain effect.

05
Load function

Load responses are linear, not saturating

Takeaway In IPS0–IPS4/5, SPL1, FEF, LO and dorsal attention A, a load function decisively beats a flat line (summed ΔAICc 33–64; 5–8 of 8 subjects each). About 60–75% of their vertices prefer a load function, and about 95% of those increase. The winning shape is linear, not saturating: even the group-average curves choose linear. One limit: with only 7 load levels, a saturating curve can't be reliably told apart from a straight line (see the simulation below).

How this was tested, and how far 7 load levels can be trusted

The 7 load levels were fit with four models, with load rescaled to 0–1 across 10% → 46%: flat (1 parameter); linear (2); saturating exponential a + s·(1−e−x/τ), which becomes linear as τ grows (3); and Naka-Rushton a + s·xⁿ/(xⁿ+c50ⁿ) (4). The amplitude s can be negative, so decreases count too. Nonlinear parameters were grid-searched, and amplitude and offset solved by least squares. Models were compared with AICc, which penalizes extra parameters and matters with only 7 points. Fits ran (a) at every fsaverage vertex in each subject and (b) on each subject's ROI average and on the group-mean curve.

05b
Attentional response function

A compressive ARF appears only against behavioral difficulty, which fits poorly

Takeaway Whether the attentional response function looks compressive depends on how load is measured. Against the design's own axis (minority share), dorsal attention responses are linear or slightly accelerating (power exponent q ≈ 1.1–1.9). Against the strength of the evidence for the majority, on a log, contrast-like axis, q falls to about 0.5–0.8: mildly compressive, but not reliably different from linear, and no better fitting. Against behavioral difficulty, the response looks strongly compressive (q ≈ 0.2–0.35, 7–8 of 8 subjects), but that axis explains the data far less well (summed ΔAICc vs flat 14 vs 68 for the dorsal composite), because the attention network already rises from 10% to 30% load, where performance is at ceiling. Cortical modulation therefore tracks stimulus-defined demand rather than performance, and nothing indicates saturation within 10–46%.

Fitted exponent by ROI and load axis

Dorsal attention composite on each load axis

How this was tested

Each subject's ROI responses (original FS-FAST betas, hemispheres averaged) were refit against three load axes, each rescaled to 0–1 across the 7 levels: the minority share itself; evidence, log(0.4 / (0.5 − share)), which treats the shrinking majority margin like stimulus contrast on a log axis; and behavioral difficulty, the group-mean error rate of the task score (misses count as errors). The model was b = a + k·xq, with q grid-searched from 0.1 to 10 (q < 1 compressive, q = 1 linear, q > 1 accelerating) and compared with linear and flat models by AICc. Exponents are geometric means across subjects; p tests log q against 0. With 7 load levels, the model-recovery simulation in section 05 shows compressive and linear shapes are hard to tell apart, so these exponents describe shape loosely.

06
Lateralization

Load effects are right-lateralized

Takeaway Load effects are larger on the right in the dorsal attention network (+0.32%, p = .035, 6 of 8 subjects) and the control network (p = .017; only the right side is significant on its own). The right hemisphere also responds about 35% more overall, and relative to that baseline the load effects are similar on both sides (0.76 vs 0.82). So this looks like the task engaging the right hemisphere more, not a right-only load mechanism. Default mode shows no asymmetry, and V1–V3 show only a tiny one.

How this was tested

Each subject's load slope was computed separately for the left and right hemisphere of every ROI. Right vs left was tested with a paired t-test (the hemisphere × load interaction), with FDR within each atlas. Individually, IPS0 (q = .045) and Glasser IP2 (q = .046) survive FDR. Because single ROIs are noisy, the composites average each subject's per-ROI effects first. For per-ROI views, use the hemisphere control above sections 03–05.

06b
Run by run

Better runs don't recruit more attention, and IPS doesn't push the periphery down

Does a better-performed run show more activity?

Run-to-run coupling with the dorsal attention network

How this was tested

Both analyses use the run-by-run block betas from the run-order refit (section 03c), hemispheres averaged. Behavior → brain: per subject and ROI, run betas were regressed on load-level dummies, run position, and the run's flip detection rate centered within load level and scaled by its within-subject SD (detection is unaffected by the key reversal in S12/S13); the detection coefficient (% signal per SD) was t-tested across subjects. Within-load variation in detection is modest (SD 4–14 points), which limits power. Coupling: per subject, run betas were residualized on load level and run position, and residuals were correlated across runs between the dorsal attention composite and each target. Because all regions share run-wide fluctuations (arousal, global signal), the same correlation was recomputed controlling a global covariate that shares no region with either member (the Schaefer default, control and salience networks, or control and salience only for pairs involving the default network). Correlations were Fisher-z transformed and t-tested across subjects.

How this was tested

Each subject's thalamus was segmented into nuclei with FreeSurfer 8.2 (segment_subregions thalamus) and grouped into LGN, MGN, pulvinar, mediodorsal, lateral posterior, anterior, ventral and intralaminar groups. Nuclei were mapped into each run's motion-corrected, unsmoothed native 2 mm data through the session registration, computing for every functional voxel the fraction lying inside each nucleus; ROI time series average voxels at least 50% inside (alternatives: ≥ 25%, fraction-weighted, and for LGN/MGN the Jülich atlas probability instead of the segmentation). Each run was fit as in the run-order analysis (SPM HRF, quadratic drift, global mean 100), hemispheres averaged; the load effect is the per-subject slope × 0.36, t-tested across the 8 subjects, with and without run position. P values are not corrected across the 9 ROIs. The attentional-response-function fits (flat, linear and power-law on the three load axes) were repeated on each nucleus.

07
Interpretation

Interpretation and open questions

Where things stand

Making a small central judgment harder recruits the dorsal attention network in a graded, roughly linear way. It also raises activity in the visual cortex that represents the task ring and lowers activity in the default mode network. That fits the "upstairs" half of the story well, and it survives a run-by-run refit that controls run order. The response grows linearly with the minority share; it looks saturating only if load is measured by behavioral difficulty, a description the data fit poorly.

The "downstairs" half is modest and spatially broad. Responses fall from about 3° outward, by about 0.4% at 6–9° (vs +0.5% in the fovea), and the decrease extends beyond the annulus into cortex the stimulus barely drives. That looks like a general center-surround redistribution rather than suppression of the ignored stimulus. Run to run, IPS activity does not trade off against the periphery: once shared fluctuations are removed, the two still rise together, and only the default-mode network trends opposite. The dual-task psychophysics agrees that the peripheral cost is modest.

What could still change the picture

  • Arousal and effort. Harder runs may raise general arousal as well as attention; the run-order control rules out slow drift, not condition-specific arousal.
  • Disengagement at 46%. Subjects miss about half the flips at 46%, which may mean they partly give up. That could explain the dip at the hardest level.
  • Stimulus differences. The foveal increase could partly reflect the different red/blue mixes or the number of button presses, not attention alone.

Next steps

  • Thalamus at higher resolution. The LGN is only about 28 functional voxels here and its boundary is uncertain; a dedicated LGN localizer and smaller voxels would be needed to test load effects there.
  • Map the surround. Use the full polar-angle and eccentricity pRF maps to test whether the peripheral decrease is uniform around the visual field or follows the attended ring's geometry.
  • Test saturation directly with more load levels between 44% and 50%, where a compressive attentional response function would flatten.
  • Link to behavior in the periphery. The earlier dual-task psychophysics study is now analyzed in the Psychophysics tab: peripheral tilt thresholds rise modestly with central load, in line with the weak peripheral suppression here.

Appendix

Methods, data checks & provenance

  • Data & label audit. Each scan run's condition file (CAM.par) was checked against the ratio stored in its behavioral file: of runs match. Raw f.nii, motion-corrected and surface-sampled data exist for every run, so a full rerun from raw data is possible. Preprocessing and GLM settings were confirmed from the original FS-FAST configs: fsaverage surface, 5 mm FWHM smoothing, SPM HRF, 16 s blocks, TR 2 s.
  • Run-order control. Run-by-run refit with run position, scan day and (in a variant) motion regressors; see section 03c. Code: scripts/glm_runpos.py (cluster batch job, glm_runpos.qsub).
  • Demand-axis fits. Power-law fits of ROI responses against ratio, evidence and behavioral-difficulty axes; see section 05b. Code: scripts/arf_demand.py.
  • Two display setups. S1–S11 were scanned in 2016–17 (41.9 cm screen at 104 cm), and S12 and S13 in 2018 on a newer setup (51.2 cm at 82 cm). The 2018 setup differs in square count (185 vs 173), red luminance (RGB 255 vs 185), noise refresh and orientation, and button mapping. Stimulus sizes in degrees match, but setup is confounded with these two subjects. Their load effects look like everyone else's, as the per-subject dots in the ROI chart show.
  • Units. FS-FAST betas after normalization to a mean of 100, so roughly % signal change relative to fixation. Wang IPS4 and IPS5 are merged (IPS5 is only 18–53 vertices).
  • Model comparison power. With 7 levels and per-subject noise, AICc is conservative. Foveal V1–V3 pass the group slope test but count as "flat" by per-subject AICc, because the effect is consistent across subjects but small relative to each subject's noise. Vertex maps (ΔAICc, τ, number of subjects with ΔAICc > 2) are saved as fsaverage .mgz files on the cluster.
  • Task reconstruction. The animation in section 01 is built from the original experiment code and the parameters stored in the behavior files. The noise updates more slowly than in the scanner unless "Real noise speed" is on.
  • Code. scripts/ contains the label audit, ROI extraction, pRF resampling, model fits, recovery simulation and this page's data build.
