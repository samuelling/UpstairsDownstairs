Central load × attention networks

Reanalysis of the NJP central-load fMRI study: 8 subjects and 151 analyzed task runs. Subjects judge which color is in the majority in a small central ring of red and blue squares. Load is the minority proportion, from 10% (easy) to 46% (nearly 50/50, hard). A task-irrelevant noise annulus fills the periphery (3–7.5°). Responses come from the original per-subject FS-FAST GLMs on fsaverage.

What was done, and the TL;DR of each analysis

  1. Data & label audit: checked each run's condition file against the ratio saved in its behavioral file, and confirmed raw and preprocessed data exist for every run.TL;DR: all 151 runs are correctly labelled, and the data are complete enough to rerun everything.
  2. Behavior: split performance into detecting the color change and naming the new majority color correctly, and fixed the scoring for the two subjects who pressed reversed keys.TL;DR: the load manipulation works in all 8 subjects. Harder ratios mostly mean more missed changes (100% → 53% detected).
  3. Dorsal attention gradient: averaged responses within Wang 2015 IPS0–IPS4/5, SPL1 and FEF, and fit each subject's slope against load.TL;DR: every dorsal ROI rises with load (t = 4.6–8.1). IPS2 rises most, about +1.2% signal.
  4. Across atlases: repeated the slope test in Glasser HCP-MMP1 and Schaefer 400/17 parcels.TL;DR: the same IPS/SPL/FEF increase appears in every atlas. The default mode network goes down with load.
  5. Fovea vs periphery: split V1–V3 by each subject's own pRF eccentricity (template for S9) into the central-task zone and the noise-annulus zone.TL;DR: foveal V1–V3 go up with load. The periphery goes down, but significantly only in V2. Subject pRFs agree with the template.
  6. Load-function models: fit flat, linear, saturating-exponential and Naka-Rushton models, vertex by vertex and on ROI averages, and compared them with AICc.TL;DR: load functions beat a flat line in IPS, SPL, FEF and LO. Their shape looks linear, not compressive, over 10–46%. Seven load levels can't support a 4-parameter Naka-Rushton.

1 · Data & label audit

What was done: each scan run's condition file (CAM.par) was compared with the ratio stored in that run's behavioral file. The preprocessing and GLM settings were also confirmed from the original FS-FAST configs: fsaverage surface, 5 mm FWHM smoothing, SPM HRF, 16 s blocks, TR 2 s.

TL;DR: of runs match. Raw f.nii, motion-corrected and surface-sampled data exist for every run, so a full rerun from raw data is possible.

2 · Behavior

What was done: after each majority-color flip, subjects had 1.5 s to press the key for the new majority color. The data were scored three ways: the original score (correct / all flips, so misses count as wrong), detection (% of flips answered), and accuracy (% correct among answered flips). S12 and S13 pressed reversed keys in every run (raw accuracy 0–46%), so their mapping is flipped. Misses stay wrong, as in the original code.

TL;DR: the load manipulation works. The original score falls from about 95% to 33% between the 30% and 46% ratios, mostly because subjects miss more changes (detection 100% → 53%). Accuracy on the changes they do catch drops less (96% → 66%).

3 · Dorsal attention gradient: IPS0 → FEF

What was done: responses were averaged within Wang 2015 maximum-probability ROIs, with hemispheres averaged. Each subject's response was regressed on ratio, and the slopes were t-tested across subjects. The "fitted" value is the slope × 0.36, the predicted change from 10% to 46%. Units are roughly % signal change vs fixation. X positions are evenly spaced, but the ratios are not; the fits use the true ratios.

TL;DR: all 7 dorsal ROIs increase with load. The effect is largest in IPS2–IPS3 and smallest in FEF. Most areas peak at 44% and dip slightly at 46%, where subjects miss half of the changes.

5 · Early visual cortex: fovea vs periphery

What was done: each subject's pRF eccentricity, R² and V1–V3 labels were resampled to fsaverage with FreeSurfer 6. Each area was split into a fovea band (0.5–1.5°, the central task) and a periphery band (3–7.5°, the noise annulus), keeping vertices with pRF R² > 10%. S9 has no pRF data, so the Benson 2014 template within Wang V1–V3 stands in for that subject. The "Template" tab uses the template for everyone.

TL;DR: foveal V1–V3 rise with load (p = .003–.007). Peripheral V1–V3 all trend down, but only V2 reaches p < .05, and it doesn't survive FDR. Subject pRFs reproduce the template result.

6 · Load-function models: monotonic vs flat

What was done: the 7 load levels were refit with four models, with load rescaled to 0–1 across 10%→46%: flat (1 parameter); linear (2); saturating exponential a + s·(1−e−x/τ), a compressive function that becomes linear as τ grows (3); and Naka-Rushton a + s·xⁿ/(xⁿ+c50ⁿ) (4). The amplitude s can be negative, so decreases count too. Nonlinear parameters were grid-searched, and amplitude and offset solved by least squares. Models were compared with AICc, which penalizes parameters and matters with only 7 points. The fits ran (a) at every fsaverage vertex in each subject and (b) on each subject's ROI average and on the group-mean curve.

TL;DR: a load function beats a flat line decisively in IPS0–IPS4/5, SPL1, FEF, LO and dorsal attention A (summed ΔAICc 33–64; 5–8 of 8 subjects each). About 60–75% of their vertices prefer a load function, and about 95% increase with load. The winning shape is linear, not compressive: even the group-mean curves choose linear (exponential τ runs to the linear limit). Simulation shows 7 levels can reliably detect a load function, but can't separate compressive from linear unless noise is low, and never select Naka-Rushton.

Caveats & provenance

  • Load is confounded with run position. Each run has one ratio, and each subject's ratio order repeats a single shuffle, so slow drift within a session could look like a load effect. Separating the two needs a GLM refit with run position as a covariate. That's the next step.
  • Behavior drives some of the 46% dip. At 46%, subjects miss about half the changes, which may mean they disengage. That would explain why the attention ROIs peak at 44%.
  • Foveal increases could partly reflect stimulus or motor differences between ratios (the red/blue mix, the number of responses).
  • Model comparison power: with 7 levels and per-subject noise, AICc is conservative. Foveal V1–V3 pass the group slope test but are "flat" by per-subject AICc, because the effect is consistent across subjects but small relative to each subject's noise. Vertex maps (ΔAICc, τ, number of subjects with ΔAICc > 2) are saved as fsaverage .mgz files on the cluster.
  • Units: FS-FAST betas after normalization to a mean of 100, so roughly % signal change relative to fixation. Wang IPS4 and IPS5 are merged (IPS5 is only 18–53 vertices).
