What should the next experiment be?

A design sketch, not data. The task moves into one hemifield and becomes a judgment about orientation. The other hemifield holds an ignored stimulus that shares the task's features, and its contrast-response function is measured at each load. Proposal

Put the task in one hemifield and measure an ignored, feature-matched probe in the other

Modelled on the hemifield design of Foster & Ling (2022). Stimulus sizes, contrasts, timings and load levels shown here are illustrative and have not been checked against that paper's Methods.

  1. Task side: a field of gaborlets at 45° and 135°. The observer judges whether the cued orientation is in the majority, the orientation analogue of the red/blue numerosity task. Load is the mixture: the closer to 50/50, the harder.
  2. Probe side: the same elements, one orientation, never relevant. Its orientation either matches the cued orientation or is orthogonal to it, and its contrast varies from event to event.
  3. One probe, two measurements. The mismatched probe gives the cost of load on an ignored stimulus. Match minus mismatch gives the spread of feature-based attention.
  4. The two accounts predict opposite signs. Load theory predicts a smaller probe response as load rises; a stronger feature template predicts a larger match advantage.
  5. The stimulus in the measured cortex never changes with load. Load order is randomized within a run, and the hardest level is titrated to stay above floor.
Part one

The reason

The existing study answers whether the attention network scales with load. It is poorly placed to say what that does to visual cortex.

01
Motivation

What the current design cannot tell us

Takeaway In the existing task the ignored stimulus shares nothing with the attended one, so the only effect it can show is a withdrawal of resources, and that effect is small. A design in which the ignored stimulus carries the attended feature can measure withdrawal and feature-based spread separately.
  • The noise annulus has no attended feature. The central task is a color judgment; the peripheral noise carries no color signal. Feature-based attention has no route to the periphery, so the design tests only spatial or resource withdrawal.
  • The measured effect is modest and spatially broad. On the fMRI tab, peripheral responses fall by about 0.4% at 6–9°, extending beyond the annulus, and IPS activity does not trade off against the periphery from run to run.
  • Load changes the stimulus at fixation. The red/blue ratio is both the difficulty manipulation and a change in the display, so foveal load effects cannot be attributed to attention alone.
  • One load per run, in a fixed order. Load was confounded with run position and had to be separated after the fact.
  • The hardest loads sit near floor. Responses dip at the 46% ratio in both the fMRI and the psychophysics, which is difficult to distinguish from disengagement.
  • One number per load. A single response amplitude cannot distinguish a baseline shift from a change in contrast gain or response gain. A contrast-response function can.
Part two

The design

Attend one hemifield, measure the other.

02
Paradigm

A gaborlet majority task on one side, an ignored probe on the other

On the task side, a field of small Gabor patches is redrawn several times a second. Each patch is tilted 45° or 135°. A line at fixation cues one orientation for the block, and the observer tracks whether that orientation is currently in the majority, reporting each time it changes. Load is set by the mixture: 30/70 is easy, 46/54 is hard. Because the cued share is balanced around 50%, the average display is the same at every load.

On the probe side, the same patches, at the same spatial frequency, all share one orientation. The probe is never a target. When the cued orientation equals the probe orientation the probe is matched; when the observer attends the other orientation it is mismatched. The probe sits at a steady adapting contrast and steps briefly to a different contrast every few seconds, so that a full contrast-response function is measured for each attention condition and load.

03
Schedule

One run: load and cue change by block, probe contrast by event

Takeaway Attention condition and load are blocked and shuffled within every run, so neither is tied to run position. Probe contrast is fast event-related: brief steps away from the adapting contrast at jittered intervals, with no-change events as the baseline.

How the budget is counted

A run is twelve 24-s blocks after a 12-s lead-in (5 minutes). Each block is one combination of cued orientation (matched or mismatched to the probe) and load, and every combination appears equally often. Probe events fall at the chosen mean spacing throughout the task blocks and are divided equally among the probe contrasts, one of which is a no-change event. "Events per cell" is the number of events at one probe contrast, in one attention condition, at one load. Cells with fewer than about 20 events will give noisy contrast-response fits for single subjects.

Part three

The predictions

What the probe's contrast-response function should do under each account.

04
Downstairs

Load cost and feature spread come apart in the same probe

Takeaway A load cost moves the matched and mismatched curves together. A change in feature-based spread moves them apart. The two can be read independently, which the current single-amplitude design does not allow.

How the schematic is built

Each curve is R(c) = b + Rmax·c²/(c² + c50²), with c50 = 20% and Rmax = 1 for the mismatched probe at easy load. Matching the cued orientation lowers c50 (a contrast-gain increase, the form Foster & Ling reported in V1–V3). A load cost is applied to both curves as a lower Rmax, a higher c50, or a lower baseline, depending on the selected form. The sizes of these changes are arbitrary and chosen for legibility.

05
Upstairs

What the same experiment asks of IPS

  • Does IPS amplitude follow load when the task is an orientation judgment? In the existing data IPS rises with load in a color task. The same function here would show it is not specific to that stimulus.
  • Does the feature signal in IPS strengthen with load? The cued orientation can be decoded from IPS activity patterns at each load. Amplitude and decoding need not move together, and it is decoding that should predict the size of the match advantage in V1–V3.
  • Does the IPS asymmetry follow the task side? The existing load effect is larger in right IPS. With the task in one hemifield, and the side alternated across sessions, a lateralized control signal can be separated from a fixed right-hemisphere bias.
  • Does IPS predict the probe, block by block? With load shuffled within runs, block-to-block coupling between IPS and the probe response is free of run-order effects by design.
06
To decide

Decisions still open

  • What "match" means. A plain majority judgment attends both orientations. The sketch above cues one orientation per block so that match and mismatch are defined. An alternative is a third probe orientation (0° or 90°) that is in neither set.
  • Load levels. Three levels are proposed in place of seven, with the hardest titrated per subject to roughly 70–75% correct so that it is not confounded with giving up.
  • Task side. Fixed within a session so that the probe location is never somewhere the observer has recently attended; alternated across sessions.
  • Probe form. A gaborlet field mirrors the task side and matches it in spatial frequency. A single grating patch would be closer to Foster & Ling and gives a cleaner retinotopic footprint.
  • Adaptation parameters. The adapting contrast, the probe contrasts, the event duration and the top-up scheme should be taken from Foster & Ling's Methods. The values shown here (16% adapter; 2–96% probes; 1-s events) are stand-ins.
  • Surround effects on the task side. A 30/70 field is more uniform in orientation than a 46/54 field, so iso-orientation surround suppression differs with load there. This does not reach the probe hemisphere, which is why the probe, not the task field, is the measurement.
  • Fine versus coarse discrimination. Scolari et al. (2012) found that hard fine discriminations move gain to off-target orientations. Keeping the judgment at 45° versus 135° at every load avoids this.
  • Fixation. A lateralized task pulls gaze toward the task side. Eye tracking in the scanner is required, and trials with gaze shifts should be excluded.
