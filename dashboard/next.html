Upstairs/Downstairs

What should the next experiment be?

A design sketch, not data. The task moves into one hemifield and becomes a judgment about orientation. The other hemifield holds an ignored stimulus that shares the task's features, and its contrast-response function is measured at each load, using the contrast-adaptation, rapid event-related procedure of Foster & Ling (2022).

Proposal

Put the task in one hemifield and measure an ignored, feature-matched probe in the other

Stimulus extent, probe contrasts, adaptation and trial timing follow Foster & Ling (2022). The gaborlet elements, the majority task and the load levels are new, and their values here are placeholders.

  1. Task side: a field of gaborlets at 45° and 135°. The observer judges whether the cued orientation is in the majority, the orientation analogue of the red/blue numerosity task. Load is the mixture: the closer to 50/50, the harder.
  2. Probe side: the same elements, one orientation, never relevant. Its orientation either matches the cued orientation or is orthogonal to it, and its contrast steps away from an adapted level on every trial.
  3. One probe, two measurements. The mismatched probe gives the cost of load on an ignored stimulus. Match minus mismatch gives the spread of feature-based attention.
  4. The two accounts predict opposite signs. Load theory predicts a smaller probe response as load rises; a stronger feature template predicts a larger match advantage.
  5. Adding load triples the conditions. Foster & Ling had 20 conditions and 18–20 trials of each in one session. Three load levels at the same timing need about three sessions for the same count.
Part one

The reason

The existing study answers whether the attention network scales with load. It is poorly placed to say what that does to visual cortex.

01
Motivation

What the current design cannot tell us

Takeaway In the existing task the ignored stimulus shares nothing with the attended one, so the only effect it can show is a withdrawal of resources, and that effect is small. A design in which the ignored stimulus carries the attended feature can measure withdrawal and feature-based spread separately.
  • The noise annulus has no attended feature. The central task is a color judgment; the peripheral noise carries no color signal. Feature-based attention has no route to the periphery, so the design tests only spatial or resource withdrawal.
  • The measured effect is modest and spatially broad. On the fMRI tab, peripheral responses fall by about 0.4% at 6–9°, extending beyond the annulus, and IPS activity does not trade off against the periphery from run to run.
  • Load changes the stimulus at fixation. The red/blue ratio is both the difficulty manipulation and a change in the display, so foveal load effects cannot be attributed to attention alone.
  • One load per run, in a fixed order. Load was confounded with run position and had to be separated after the fact.
  • The hardest loads sit near floor. Responses dip at the 46% ratio in both the fMRI and the psychophysics, which is difficult to distinguish from disengagement.
  • One number per load. A single response amplitude cannot distinguish a baseline shift from a change in contrast gain or response gain. A contrast-response function can.
Part two

The design

Attend one hemifield, measure the other.

02
Paradigm

A gaborlet majority task on one side, an ignored probe on the other

Each hemifield holds a wedge of Gabor patches spanning 1° to 8.5° of eccentricity and 70° of polar angle above and below the horizontal meridian, the stimulus extent Foster & Ling used for their gratings. On the task side each patch is tilted 45° or 135°. A line at fixation cues one orientation, the field appears for 4 s, and the observer then reports whether the cued orientation was in the majority. Load is the mixture: 10/90 is easy, 42/58 is hard. The cued share is balanced around 50%, so the average display is the same at every load.

On the probe side the patches all share one orientation, and the probe is never a target. When the cued orientation equals the probe orientation the probe is matched; otherwise it is mismatched. The probe is on continuously at 16% contrast, to which the observer is adapted, and during each trial it steps to one of ten contrasts. Patch size and spacing grow with eccentricity to limit crowding, and the two sides use identical elements, so they are matched in spatial frequency at every eccentricity.

03
Schedule

One run: adapt for a minute, then forty 4-s trials

Takeaway The timing is Foster & Ling's: 60 s of adaptation to the probe at 16% contrast, then a 456-s rapid event-related sequence of 4-s trials separated by 6–17 s of top-up adaptation. Cue, load and probe contrast all change from trial to trial, so none of them is tied to run position.

Sessions
How the budget is counted

A run is 516 s (60 s adaptation + 456 s of trials) and holds 40 trials, as in Foster & Ling. A condition is one probe contrast in one attention condition (matched or mismatched) at one load. Trials per condition = 40 × runs × sessions ÷ conditions. Foster & Ling ran 2 attention conditions × 10 contrasts = 20 conditions for 9 or 10 runs, giving 18–20 trials per condition; set load levels to 1 to reproduce that. With more than 40 conditions a single run cannot contain every condition, so the sequence has to be balanced across runs.

Part three

The predictions

What the probe's contrast-response function should do under each account.

04
Downstairs

Load cost and feature spread come apart in the same probe

Takeaway A load cost moves the matched and mismatched curves together. A change in feature-based spread moves them apart. The two can be read independently, which the current single-amplitude design does not allow.

How the schematic is built

Each curve is the Naka–Rushton function Foster & Ling fitted, R(c) = b + Gr·cn/(cn + Gcn), in z-scored BOLD units. The medium-load panel uses values close to their V1 estimates: baseline b = −0.24, semisaturation 39% contrast for the mismatched probe and 28% for the matched probe, exponent 1.2, and Gr = 0.9, which gives a response range near their Rmax of 0.7. The response is near zero at the 16% adapting contrast and negative below it because the measurement is relative to the adapted state. How these values change with load is the open question: the sizes of the load effects drawn here are arbitrary and chosen for legibility.

05
Upstairs

What the same experiment asks of IPS

  • Does IPS amplitude follow load when the task is an orientation judgment? In the existing data IPS rises with load in a color task. The same function here would show it is not specific to that stimulus.
  • Does the feature signal in IPS strengthen with load? The cued orientation can be decoded from IPS activity patterns at each load. Amplitude and decoding need not move together, and it is decoding that should predict the size of the match advantage in V1–V3.
  • Does the IPS asymmetry follow the task side? The existing load effect is larger in right IPS. With the task in one hemifield, and the side alternated across sessions, a lateralized control signal can be separated from a fixed right-hemisphere bias.
  • Does IPS predict the probe, trial by trial? With load varying from trial to trial, coupling between IPS and the probe response is free of run-order effects by design.
06
To decide

Decisions still open

  • Crowding. Densely packed peripheral gaborlets crowd one another, and the majority judgment becomes hard even at lopsided mixtures. The sketch scales element size and spacing with eccentricity (radial spacing about 0.36 × eccentricity, 48 elements). The costs are fewer elements, so a coarser set of possible mixtures, and a spatial frequency that falls with eccentricity instead of a uniform 2.2 cycles/deg.
  • What "match" means. A plain majority judgment attends both orientations. The sketch cues one orientation per trial so that match and mismatch are defined. An alternative is a third probe orientation (0° or 90°) that is in neither set.
  • Cueing a feature, not a place. Foster & Ling cued one of two gratings by location, and the attended feature followed from which grating that was. Here the two orientations are intermixed and the cue names the orientation directly.
  • Load levels. Three are proposed in place of seven. The mixtures shown (10/90, 30/70, 42/58) are guesses; the hardest should be titrated per subject to roughly 70–75% correct so that it is not confounded with giving up. Foster & Ling staircased to about 77%.
  • Task-side contrast. Foster & Ling showed the task gratings at 16%, the adapting contrast. The sketch draws the task gaborlets at 50% so the mixture is visible; whether that changes adaptation in the task hemisphere matters little, since the measurement is in the other one.
  • Task side. Foster & Ling counterbalanced it across observers. To test whether the IPS asymmetry follows the task, it has to alternate within observer, across sessions, and stay fixed within a session so the probe location is never recently attended.
  • Probe form. A gaborlet field mirrors the task side element for element. A grating wedge is what Foster & Ling used and gives a stronger, more uniform drive.
  • Fine versus coarse discrimination. Scolari et al. (2012) found that hard fine discriminations move gain to off-target orientations. Keeping the judgment at 45° versus 135° at every load avoids this.
  • Fixation. A lateralized task pulls gaze toward the task side. Foster & Ling tracked eye position in the scanner and showed it did not differ between attention conditions; the same check is needed across load.
