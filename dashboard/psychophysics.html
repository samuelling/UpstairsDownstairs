Upstairs/Downstairs

Does a harder judgment at fixation cost perception in the periphery?

A reanalysis of the dual-task psychophysics study that preceded the fMRI experiment: 10 subjects, 300 runs, 48,000 trials. On every trial a 50-ms display showed 200 red and blue squares at fixation and a tilted grating in the periphery, and subjects reported both the majority color and the tilt. A QUEST staircase measured the tilt threshold separately at each central load.

Summary

Central load costs peripheral orientation sensitivity a little, and not in everyone

Main analyses use the 7 subjects who met the original study's reliability criteria; all 10 are shown as a check. There is no single-task baseline, so these are costs relative to easy central loads, not relative to no central task.

  1. The central manipulation works. Majority-color accuracy falls from 97% at an 18% minority share to 61% at 48%, in every subject.
  2. Peripheral tilt thresholds rise modestly. Thresholds are about 1.3× higher at hard (44–48%) than easy (18–36%) loads; 6 of 7 subjects rise, but the group effect is not significant (p = .08).
  3. The cost is concentrated in a few subjects. Three subjects' thresholds rise 1.6–2×; the others are essentially flat.
  4. The two tasks succeed together. Holding tilt constant, a peripheral judgment is more likely correct when the central judgment on the same trial was correct (7 of 7 subjects).
  5. Costs dip at the hardest load. Thresholds peak at 44–46% and fall back at 48%, where central accuracy nears chance, echoing the fMRI dip at 46%.

Part one

The experiment

Two judgments from one brief glimpse: a numerosity judgment at fixation, an orientation judgment in the periphery.

01
Paradigm

A dual task from a single 50-ms display

Each trial flashed, for 50 ms, 200 small squares scattered over a grid around fixation (within about 2° of the center), each red or blue, together with a 2 cycle/deg, 40%-contrast grating filling an annulus from about 5.5° to 12° eccentricity. The grating was tilted slightly left or right of vertical. In the following 2.5 s, subjects reported which color was in the majority (with a tone after errors) and which way the grating was tilted.

Central load was set by the minority share, from 18% (easy) to 48% (nearly even). Each run interleaved two load levels, both majority colors, and 40 trials of each, with a separate QUEST staircase per condition adjusting the tilt to reach 80% correct. The tilt threshold therefore measures peripheral orientation sensitivity at each central load.

Manipulation check

Central accuracy falls steeply with load

Takeaway Majority-color accuracy drops from about 97% at an 18% minority share to 61% at 48% (−31 points across the range, t(6) = −9.8; all 10 subjects show the drop). Unlike the fMRI task, subjects almost never skipped a response, so the cost appears as errors rather than misses.

Central accuracy

Peripheral accuracy (held near 80% by QUEST)

How this was measured

Central accuracy is the proportion of trials with the correct majority color, pooling both majority colors (each load level was tested with blue and with red in the majority). Peripheral accuracy is plotted to show that the staircases did their job: QUEST adjusted tilt so that accuracy stayed near 80% at every load, which is why the threshold, not accuracy, carries the load effect.

Downstairs

Peripheral tilt thresholds rise modestly with central load

Tilt threshold by central load

Pooled psychometric functions, easy vs hard load

How this was tested

Two threshold estimates were computed for each subject and load. The psychometric fit pools all trials at a load and fits a Weibull function (guess rate 50%) with one slope and one lapse rate per subject and a separate threshold per load; the threshold is the Weibull scale parameter, the tilt giving about 82% correct in the absence of lapses. The QUEST staircase estimate is the median across runs of each staircase's final estimate. Thresholds are averaged geometrically (in log units), so a few very high values don't dominate. Load effects are tested per subject as the change in log threshold from 18% to 48% (regression across all tested loads) and as the hard (44–48%) vs easy (18–36%) ratio, then t-tested across subjects.

Trial by trial

Peripheral judgments are better when the central judgment succeeds

Peripheral accuracy by central outcome

Logistic model of peripheral correctness

How this was tested

For each subject, a logistic regression predicted whether the tilt judgment was correct from the log tilt on that trial, the central load, and whether the central judgment on the same trial was correct. Because QUEST sets the tilt, raw accuracy split by central outcome (left chart) is only approximate; the model coefficient for central correctness (right chart) is the cleaner estimate because it holds tilt constant. Coefficients are in log-odds and were t-tested across subjects.

06
Pilot · attentional response function

In two pilot subjects, central load scales down the peripheral contrast response

Takeaway Before the tilt study, two subjects did a contrast-discrimination version of the dual task: two peripheral gratings, one at a pedestal contrast, and a judgment of which was higher. Measuring thresholds across pedestals (a TvC function) at easy and hard central loads shows how load acts on the underlying contrast response function. In both subjects, and in every pilot set that reaches hard loads, a response-gain change (lower Rmax) fits clearly better than a contrast-gain change (shifted c50): from 5% to 48% load, Rmax falls by about a quarter (×0.76 in both subjects), and by about half across 12–48% in subject B's wider-range set. Hard load raises thresholds most at the highest pedestal and least in the dip. A narrower 28–44% set from subject A shows the same direction but no reliable effect. These are pilot data from two subjects, one of them the experimenter, so they suggest a mechanism rather than establish one.
How this was tested

Each trial flashed the central red/blue squares (100 or 200) for 50 ms together with two 6 cycle/deg gratings left and right of fixation, one at a fixed pedestal contrast and one at a higher test contrast; subjects reported the majority color and which grating had higher contrast. QUEST tracked the test contrast separately for each blue-square count; the threshold is the final estimate minus the pedestal (staircases pinned at the 99% contrast ceiling were dropped). Pedestals varied across runs, and each run interleaved two loads, each with both majority colors. Central accuracy fell from about 100% at a 5% minority share to 54–58% at 48%. The model is a Naka-Rushton contrast response R(c) = Rmax·cⁿ/(cⁿ + c50ⁿ); the threshold ΔC is the increment that raises R by one unit, which produces the dipper-shaped TvC. All staircases' log thresholds were fit with one exponent n per set and four hypotheses about load: none, contrast gain (c50 depends on load), response gain (Rmax depends on load), or both. With two loads the load-dependent parameter is free; with more, it is log-linear in load. Models were compared by AICc. Caveats: two subjects; pedestal changes across runs, so it is partly confounded with session order; and response gain vs contrast gain inferred from TvC data depends on the assumed noise (here constant).

07
Interpretation

Interpretation and relation to the fMRI

Where things stand

Raising central load sharply impairs the central judgment but only modestly impairs the concurrent peripheral orientation judgment. Thresholds rise by about a third on average, consistently in direction but not significantly, and the cost is concentrated in a subset of subjects.

This lines up with the fMRI: there, foveal visual cortex rose with load while peripheral suppression was weak. Both datasets point to a limited, variable cost to the periphery rather than a strong push-pull trade-off. The contrast-discrimination pilots (section 06) suggest what form that cost takes: a reduction in response gain, which mainly affects high-contrast stimuli such as the fMRI noise annulus, and both show the effect dipping at the hardest load, where central performance approaches chance and subjects may stop trying.

Caveats

  • No single-task baseline. Costs are relative to easy central loads; the overall cost of doing two tasks at once can't be estimated.
  • Unreliable staircases. Three subjects often could not do the tilt task at all (many thresholds above 20°), so they are set aside in the main analysis, as in the original study.
  • Small sample and heterogeneity. With 7 subjects and a 2-fold range of individual effects, the study is underpowered for a cost of this size.
  • Trial-level coupling is ambiguous. Joint success may reflect momentary alertness or lapses that affect both tasks, not a trade-off between them.

Possible next steps

  • Confirm the response-gain result. Run the contrast-discrimination version in more subjects, with pedestals interleaved within runs and a single-task baseline.
  • Model the lapses directly. Fit lapse rate per load to ask whether load causes all-or-none failures in the periphery rather than graded sensitivity loss.
  • Relate to the fMRI subjects. If any subjects did both studies, compare their behavioral costs with their peripheral fMRI responses.
  • Power analysis. Use the observed effect and variability to size a confirmatory study with a single-task baseline.

Appendix

Methods, data checks & provenance

  • Data. Subjects are coded P01–P10; the code key stays on the cluster. Five subjects were tested at 8 loads (18, 28, 36, 40, 42, 44, 46, 48%) and five at 16 loads (18–48% in 2% steps); group curves use the 8 shared loads, and per-subject load effects use every tested load.
  • Display. 1280 × 1024 screen, 55 cm wide at 57 cm; squares 3 px (about 0.13°) on an 18 × 18 grid within about 2° of fixation; grating 2 cycles/deg nominal, 40% contrast, vertical ± tilt; 50 ms per trial, 2.5 s response window.
  • Reliability criteria (from the original analysis script): a run × condition staircase is usable if central accuracy is at least 50% and the final threshold is at most 20°; a subject is included if more than 70% of staircases are usable. Seven subjects pass.
  • Original pipeline. The original figures averaged the usable staircase thresholds arithmetically. That pipeline is reproduced below and gives the same pattern: thresholds rise from about 2.7° to 4.2° at 44% and fall back at 48%.
  • Run order. Load pairs were cycled in a fixed shuffled order, so load and run position are partly confounded. Including run number in the regression leaves the load effect similar (QUEST log-threshold slope p = .07), and thresholds show no reliable practice trend.
  • Code. scripts/psy_analysis.py (cluster analysis) and scripts/build_psy_data.py (this page's data).
